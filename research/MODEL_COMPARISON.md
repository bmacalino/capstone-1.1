# Model comparison protocol and results

Status: sentiment comparison complete; transformer fine-tuning and hazard
comparison pending. Generated by `research/compare_sentiment_models.py`.
Machine-readable results: `research/sentiment_model_comparison.json`.

This document exists because the earlier transformer comparison
(`research/compare_transformers.py`) recorded its results only as JSON, with no
written protocol. It also addresses two weaknesses in that earlier comparison:
the transformers were evaluated off the shelf rather than fine-tuned, and no
significance test was reported.

---

## 1. Research question

Does the deployed TF-IDF and logistic regression sentiment classifier
outperform alternative techniques on Filipino and Taglish e-commerce reviews,
and is any observed difference statistically distinguishable from chance?

## 2. Data

| Split | File | Rows | Distribution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Train | `FiReCS_train_set.csv` | 7,340 → 7,334 after cleaning | neg 2,381 / neu 2,547 / pos 2,406 |
| Test | `FiReCS_test_set.csv` | 3,147 | neg 1,027 / neu 1,087 / pos 1,033 |

Source: FiReCS (CC BY 4.0), `huggingface.co/datasets/ccosme/FiReCS`. Both files
are integrity-pinned by SHA-256 in `sentiment-analysis/data/firecs/README.md`
and were verified unchanged before this run.

Cleaning, performed identically for every arm by
`train_firecs.audit_training_records`: 2 exact duplicate training rows removed,
and 4 training rows removed for appearing in the test split. Removing the
latter matters — leaving them in would inflate every arm's score equally and
invisibly.

Test labels were used for reporting only. No arm selected a hyperparameter,
threshold, or epoch using them.

## 3. Fairness controls

The central threat to a multi-model comparison is that arms differ in more than
the algorithm. Three controls:

1. **Identical rows.** Every arm trains on the same 7,334 cleaned rows.
2. **Identical text.** Every classical arm receives
   `feature_text(DefaketiveSentimentModel.normalize(review))` — the exact
   preprocessing the deployed model uses, including Taglish normalization and
   the six appended feature-marker tokens. The lexicon arm is the deliberate
   exception: it reads raw text because it performs its own normalization and
   the marker tokens would be noise to it. Each arm's input is recorded in the
   `text_input` field of the results JSON.
3. **Fixed seed.** Seed 42 throughout.

**Reproduction check.** The comparison includes the shipped `.joblib` artifact
the application loads today as its own arm. It scored accuracy 0.8112 and macro
F1 0.8138, reproducing the recorded values exactly, and McNemar's test against
the retrained incumbent found **zero discordant predictions** — the two agree on
all 3,147 rows. The harness therefore feeds models the same text the training
script did, and the remaining numbers can be read as algorithm differences.

## 4. Metrics

Primary metric is **macro F1**, chosen because all three classes matter equally
and the test split is near-balanced. Reported alongside it:

- 95% percentile bootstrap confidence interval, 2,000 resamples.
- **McNemar's test** against the incumbent. This is the correct test for two
  classifiers on one test set: it examines only the rows where the two models
  disagree, since rows both got right or both got wrong carry no evidence about
  which is better.
- **Holm-Bonferroni correction** across the six comparisons. Running six tests
  at α = 0.05 would otherwise give roughly a 26% chance of at least one false
  positive.
- **Median single-review latency** and **serialized model size**, measured
  because the model runs per-review inside a Flask application.

Implementations: `research_utils.mcnemar_test`,
`research_utils.bootstrap_macro_f1_ci`, `research_utils.holm_bonferroni`,
covered by `tests/test_research_utils.py`.

## 5. Results — sentiment

Sorted by macro F1. Adjusted p-values are Holm-corrected, against the incumbent.

| Arm | Accuracy | Macro F1 | 95% CI | p (adj.) | Latency | Size |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **TF-IDF + LogisticRegression** (deployed) | 0.8112 | **0.8138** | [0.800, 0.827] | — | **3.0 ms** | **2.5 MB** |
| TF-IDF + SVD(300) + LogisticRegression | 0.8036 | 0.8056 | [0.792, 0.819] | 0.371 | 43.3 ms | 68.7 MB |
| TF-IDF + LinearSVC | 0.7982 | 0.8004 | [0.787, 0.815] | 0.0006 | 3.0 ms | 3.1 MB |
| TF-IDF + ComplementNB | 0.7881 | 0.7871 | [0.772, 0.802] | 0.0013 | 4.1 ms | 4.9 MB |
| TF-IDF + SVD(300) + HistGradientBoosting | 0.7757 | 0.7784 | [0.764, 0.793] | <0.0001 | 83.7 ms | 72.6 MB |
| VADER lexicon (no learning) | 0.5284 | 0.4818 | [0.466, 0.497] | <0.0001 | 2.1 ms | — |

Per-class F1 for the deployed model: negative 0.8132, neutral 0.7469,
positive 0.8815. Neutral is the weakest class in every arm without exception,
which identifies it as the headroom rather than as a defect of any one method.

### Interpretation

**The deployed model is significantly better than three of the five
alternatives.** Against LinearSVC (p = 0.0006), ComplementNB (p = 0.0013) and
gradient boosting (p < 0.0001), the margin survives correction for multiple
comparisons. Against the VADER lexicon the gap is large and unambiguous:
+0.332 macro F1, with the lexicon correct on only 249 rows the trained model
missed while missing 1,139 the trained model caught.

**One result is a genuine tie, and it is the most informative one.** The SVD
arm scored 0.8056 against the incumbent's 0.8138, but McNemar's test returns
p = 0.371 after correction — the difference is not distinguishable from chance,
and the confidence intervals overlap substantially. The two models disagreed on
302 rows, splitting them 163 to 139. **The accuracy argument cannot separate
these two models.** What separates them is cost: the SVD arm is 14× slower per
review and 27× larger on disk, for no measurable accuracy gain. That is the
basis on which the simpler model is retained, and it is an engineering
justification rather than a statistical one — stated here explicitly rather
than concealed behind a marginally higher F1.

**Gradient boosting was the weakest learned arm.** Tree ensembles require dense
input, so they cannot consume the sparse TF-IDF matrix directly and must sit
behind dimensionality reduction; the 300-component bottleneck discards
discriminative sparse features. This is a property of the method on this data,
not a tuning failure.

### What was already known and is reproduced here

LinearSVC was evaluated during the original model-selection cross-validation
(0.8037 mean CV macro F1) but was never scored on the held-out test set. This
run closes that gap: 0.8004 on test, and the gap to the incumbent is
statistically significant.

## 6. Transformer arms — the fine-tuned model wins on accuracy

The earlier comparison evaluated pretrained transformers off the shelf, on a
task they were never trained for. To test the architecture rather than the
checkpoint's original training, the same XLM-R checkpoint was fine-tuned on the
same 7,334 cleaned rows (`research/finetune_transformer.py`, 3 epochs, epoch
chosen on a stratified 10% validation slice, test labels read once afterwards).

**Paired result on the identical checkpoint:**

| `cardiffnlp/twitter-xlm-roberta-base-sentiment` | Accuracy | Macro F1 | Neutral F1 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Off the shelf (zero-shot) | 0.6822 | 0.6389 | 0.3556 |
| **Fine-tuned on FiReCS** | **0.8449** | **0.8461** | **0.7788** |
| Deployed TF-IDF + LogisticRegression | 0.8112 | 0.8138 | 0.7469 |

Fine-tuning gained **+0.2072 macro F1** over the zero-shot result. This
confirms the earlier comparison understated the architecture: nearly all of the
apparent gap was the absence of task-specific training, not a limitation of
transformers on Taglish.

**Against the deployed model the fine-tuned transformer is genuinely better.**
Macro F1 0.8461 versus 0.8138, 95% CIs [0.8334, 0.8590] and [0.8002, 0.8274]
— which do not overlap — and McNemar's test gives **p = 0.000001** on 468
discordant rows split 287 to 181 in the transformer's favour. The gain is
concentrated where the deployed model is weakest: neutral F1 rises from 0.7469
to 0.7788.

This result is reported as found. The deployed model does not have the better
accuracy.

### Why the deployed model is nevertheless retained

| | TF-IDF + LogisticRegression | Fine-tuned XLM-R |
|---|---|---|
| Macro F1 | 0.8138 | 0.8461 |
| Latency per review (CPU) | **3.0 ms** | 93.4 ms |
| Latency per review (GPU) | n/a | 23.9 ms |
| Artifact size | **2.4 MB** | 1.1 GB |
| Per-word explanation of a prediction | **yes** | no |
| Requires a GPU for acceptable speed | no | in practice, yes |

The application scores every review on a product page. For a product with 100
reviews the deployed model takes about 0.3 seconds on CPU; the transformer
takes about 9.3 seconds on the same hardware, and still 2.4 seconds with a GPU
the deployment target does not have. The transformer is also 460× larger and
cannot show which words drove a prediction, which the interface currently
displays as evidence.

The decision is therefore: **+0.032 macro F1 is not worth a 31× latency
increase, a 460× size increase, a GPU requirement, and the loss of
explanations** for this application. That is an engineering judgement about
deployment constraints, not a claim that the simpler model is more accurate.
The fine-tuned transformer is retained as a documented comparison arm and as
the reference point for future work.

### Threat to validity

The transformer consumed raw review text while the classical arms consumed the
project's normalized text with feature markers. This follows standard practice
— transformer tokenizers perform their own normalization — but it means the
comparison is architecture-plus-preprocessing rather than architecture alone.
A normalized-text ablation is recorded as outstanding work.

## 6b. Attempts to improve the deployed model — three negative results

Error analysis first. Of the deployed model's 594 test errors, **570 (96%)
involve the neutral boundary** — 356 negative/neutral confusions and 214
neutral/positive. Negative and positive are confused only 24 times in 3,147
rows (<1%). The model resolves polarity well and cannot place the neutral
boundary, so representation is not the bottleneck. Three interventions were
tried and none produced a significant gain.

**(a) Hyperparameter search — no significant improvement.** No search had ever
been run in this project; the deployed settings were hand-chosen and compared
as five fixed candidates. A 40-candidate randomized search over n-gram ranges,
minimum document frequency, feature counts, sublinear scaling, `C` and class
weighting (`research/tune_sentiment_model.py`, same repeated stratified CV,
seed 42) returned best CV macro F1 0.8186 against the incumbent's 0.8157 under
identical splits. On test: 0.8169 versus 0.8138, McNemar **p = 0.399 — not
significant**. The hand-chosen configuration is at or near the optimum for this
architecture.

**(b) SentiTaglish adds nothing, and contains test data.** The corpus offered
7,113 usable three-class rows. After `audit_training_records`, **2,138 were
found in the FiReCS test split** and 4,969 more were duplicates; **12 rows
survived as new training data**, and test macro F1 was unchanged at 0.8133.
SentiTaglish and FiReCS are substantially the same underlying collection.

This is the most important operational finding in this document. Training on
SentiTaglish without the cross-split leakage check would have placed 2,138 test
reviews into training and produced an inflated, invalid test score. The check in
`train_firecs.audit_training_records` prevented it automatically. Any future
corpus must pass the same check before use.

**(c) TagaSenti degrades the model.** Adding it contributed 24,996 rows, 3.4×
the training set, and test macro F1 **fell from 0.8138 to 0.7949**. The loader
already records it as mixed-provenance, and the source counts preserve that
label (`tagasenti_mixed_provenance`). Corpus size does not compensate for
distribution mismatch.

**Consequence.** Within this architecture and this data the deployed model is
close to its ceiling. The remaining untried levers are the neutral decision
boundary and ordinal modelling, both of which target the 82% directly. Before
pursuing them, `research/score_ceiling_check.py` estimates how many of the 594
errors are fixable at all: a human labels a blind, stratified sample of 30
boundary errors without seeing either the dataset label or the model's
prediction, and the errors are partitioned into genuine model failures,
arguable dataset labels, and genuinely ambiguous reviews.

## 6c. Removing the neutral class — measured and rejected

Since 82% of errors involve neutral, dropping the class was evaluated. Retrained
as a binary positive/negative task it scores **accuracy 0.9636, macro F1 0.9636**
against the three-class 0.8112 / 0.8138.

The gain is not real. The 1,087 neutral test reviews do not cease to exist; put
through the binary model they are forced **693 negative and 394 positive**. A
third of the data is assigned a polarity it does not hold, and roughly two
thirds of it is labelled negative.

Sentiment no longer feeds any badge — `hazard_parameters_v1.json` sets
`sentiment/used_for_risk_decisions: false` — but it still drives the review
counts shown to users and the ranking of recommended alternatives by positive
ratio. Both would be corrupted: lukewarm reviews reported to shoppers as
negative, and products carrying many mild reviews unfairly demoted.

The three-class scheme is therefore retained. Should the boundary be revisited,
the defensible route is an abstention class — a statement about the model's
confidence rather than about the reviewer's opinion, for which the marketplace
path already uses the term "uncertain" — not deletion of the class.

## 7. Hazard comparison

**Update, 26 Sept 2026: labelling round 1 is complete and the tagger has been
measured.** Results, the model 0.2.0 changes, and their limits are in
`RESULTS_TABLES.md` Part B (Tables 9–12). In brief: annotator agreement κ =
0.62 safety, 0.54 defect and counterfeit; the shipped safety check catches 21 of
27 confirmed reports with 21 of 39 flags real (in-sample); no safety report
occurred in 280 randomly sampled sentences, so population recall is not
estimable from this round. The text below describes the position before
labelling, and the evaluation plan that was then followed.

Before round 1 the tagger had no human-labelled evaluation data:

- All 900 sentences in `outputs/labelling_queue_round_1/` were unlabelled.
- The only locked test set, `outputs/lazada_gold_test_v1/`, contains zero
  safety and zero counterfeit rows, and both of its recorded annotators were
  the same language-model pass, agreeing on 78 of 78 rows on every field.

Any model comparison run on those labels would have measured agreement with a
machine annotator, not accuracy. Labelling therefore preceded modelling.

Once worksheets are returned, `research/analyze_labelling_round_1.py` reports
Cohen's kappa per category, the keyword screen's precision, weighted prevalence
from the `random_all` stratum only, and an anchoring check comparing blind
against pre-labelled rows. Because expected prevalence is roughly 0.6–1.3%,
evaluation will use precision-recall AUC and precision at the recall floors
declared in `hazard_parameters_v1.json` (safety 0.80, defect 0.70,
counterfeit 0.70). Accuracy is not a meaningful metric at that prevalence and
will not be reported.

Model comparison will be scoped to **defect**, the only category with enough
candidate positives. Safety and counterfeit will be reported as a lexicon
screen with measured precision, because training a classifier on a few dozen
positives and reporting an accuracy figure would reproduce the circular
labelling problem this work exists to correct.

## 7b. Review sampling for product reports (Lazada)

Until 27 Sept 2026 a scan read the first 100 written reviews in Lazada's
default ("Relevance") order. For a popular listing that is a small,
non-random slice (100 of 13,892 on the Anker charger), dominated by 5-star
praise, and the report correctly refused a verdict because the collection
was incomplete.

Scans now use **stratified sampling by star level**: up to 60 written reviews
from each of the five levels (read to the end of a level's list when it has
fewer). The number of ratings per level, N_h, is read from the listing's own
review feed (it must sum to within 5% of the listed total). Every analysed
review from level h carries the inverse-probability weight N_h / n_h, where
n_h is the number of reviews analysed from that level, and every product rate
(safety, counterfeit, defect, sentiment shares) is the weighted share. Its 95%
Wilson interval uses Kish's effective sample size, n_eff = (Σw)² / Σw², in
place of the review count. The minimum-sample rule (30 reviews) still counts
reviews actually read. A level whose collection stopped early makes the sample
partial, which forces the insufficient-evidence verdict as before.

On the Anker listing (N = 35, 9, 42, 102, 13,704 for 1–5 stars) a scan
collected 28, 7, 27, 46 and 60 written reviews (168; 166 after removing
duplicates; n_eff = 61.7) in about two minutes, and surfaced five safety reports from low-star reviews that the
first-100 method never reached.

Limitations: N_h counts all ratings, including ratings with no text, so the
weights assume the written reviews at a level are typical of all its ratings.
A level with ratings but no written review cannot be represented and drops
out of the weighted rates. Sixty reviews rule out a problem affecting more
than about 5% of a level's reviews (rule of three), not rarer ones.

## 8. Limitations

1. FiReCS is a single-source corpus. Results may not transfer to scraped
   marketplace reviews, which are noisier.
2. The comparison is one frozen train/test split, not repeated cross-validation.
   Bootstrap intervals quantify test-set sampling variability, not
   training-set variability.
3. Latency was measured on one CPU machine and is indicative, not a benchmark.
4. No arm in the original comparison was promoted, and every result there
   carries `qualified_for_integration: false`. The deployed sentiment model was
   later replaced by a slimmed fine-tuned XLM-RoBERTa, under a rule fixed in
   advance; see `research/RESULTS_TABLES.md` Tables 8c–8d.
